Sassywood and the Transformation of Liberian Society: Rethinking Custom, Justice, and the Demands of Modern Times

A reflection on customary practice, constitutional government, human dignity, and the responsibility to shape cultural traditions in a changing society.

Theotis Luke Sneh · November 2026

Text size

1. Introduction: The Question Beyond Sassywood

Every society inherits practices, beliefs, and institutions from earlier generations. Some provide identity, solidarity, and a sense of belonging. Others may become difficult to reconcile with changing understandings of justice, human dignity, and the responsibilities of a constitutional state. The challenge facing a modern society is not whether it should remember its past, but how it should assess that past and decide which practices deserve to continue.

In Liberia, sassywood remains one of the most difficult subjects in the relationship between customary belief and formal justice. Historically associated with trial by ordeal, sassywood has been used in attempts to determine guilt, expose alleged wrongdoing, or resolve accusations through a ritual test. Its continued discussion raises a fundamental question: can a society preserve its cultural identity while rejecting practices that endanger life, compromise liberty, or deny a person the protections of law?

Liberia does not have to abandon its cultural identity to embrace modern justice. However, a society committed to progress must be willing to examine its inherited practices, preserve those that contribute positively to social cohesion, and reject those that compromise human dignity, individual liberty, and the rule of law. The case against coercive trial by ordeal is not a case against Liberian culture. It is an argument for cultural development consistent with the rights and responsibilities of a modern constitutional society.

2. Understanding Sassywood Within the Liberian Cultural Experience

Customary institutions have historically played an important role in community life in Liberia. Traditional authorities and community leaders have helped settle disagreements, restore relationships, interpret local norms, and maintain social order. In communities where access to formal institutions may be difficult, customary processes can provide a familiar and accessible forum for addressing certain disputes.

Sassywood, however, must be distinguished from customary mediation and reconciliation. Voluntary dialogue, community mediation, apology, restitution, and consensual settlement are not the same as forcing a person to ingest a substance, undergo a dangerous test, suffer physical punishment, or submit to humiliation to establish guilt or innocence. A process does not become just merely because it is traditional or accepted by some members of a community.

When an ordeal is used to compel a confession or determine guilt through physical risk, it replaces evidence and fair hearing with a dangerous test. The result may be injury, death, false accusation, or punishment without lawful adjudication. The distinction is therefore essential: the preservation of customary dispute resolution does not require the preservation of coercive ordeals.

3. The Supreme Court of Liberia and the Legal Position on Sassywood

3.1 Jedah, Boyah et al. v. Jeffrey B. Horace (1916)

In Jedah, Boyah et al. v. Jeffrey B. Horace, 2 LLR 265, 269 (1916), persons associated with a society known as the Negees were arrested following the involvement of a medicine man and were ordered by a travelling commissioner to undergo a sassywood ordeal. The affected persons sought injunctive relief. The Supreme Court reversed the lower court’s dissolution of the injunction and perpetuated the injunction against the ordeal.

The Court held, in substance, that executive officers could not assume judicial functions and that the commissioner lacked jurisdiction to order the ordeal. It also treated the use of sassywood to extract a confession as inconsistent with the protection against compelled self-incrimination under the constitutional framework then in force. The Court described the administration of sassywood in any case as illegal and a practice that should be discouraged.

The decision is significant because it addressed both the limits of official authority and the fundamental injustice of compelling a person to prove innocence through an ordeal. It demonstrates that opposition to coercive sassywood is not a recent idea imported into Liberian society; the Supreme Court had addressed the issue more than a century ago.

3.2 Tenteah et al. v. Republic of Liberia (1940)

In Tenteah et al. v. Republic of Liberia, 7 LLR 63 (1940), Flarn Yonnoh Teah was accused of stealing a goat and was subjected to a sassywood ordeal. After being identified as guilty, he was stripped, beaten, and mistreated; he later died. The defendants were prosecuted for murder.

The Supreme Court affirmed the convictions and expressly stated that trial by ordeal was unconstitutional and illegal. The Court reaffirmed the reasoning in Jedah and referred to Posum v. Pardee, 4 LLR 295, 306 (1935), in its discussion of ordeals used to extort the truth. The case illustrates the grave consequences that can follow when a community substitutes coercive ritual for lawful investigation and adjudication.

Tenteah is particularly important because the Court did not treat the ordeal as a harmless cultural ceremony. The case connected the unlawful practice to real criminal consequences and affirmed that customary belief cannot justify conduct that causes death or serious harm.

3.3 Elizabeth Walker-Nagbe et al. v. Republic of Liberia (1984)

In Elizabeth Walker-Nagbe et al. v. Republic of Liberia, 32 LLR 474 (1984), seven persons died in circumstances connected to allegations of witchcraft and a reported sassywood ordeal. The prosecution and defence offered conflicting accounts of the deaths. The Supreme Court found serious gaps and uncertainty in the evidence and reversed the conviction for a new trial so that the material facts could be properly clarified.

The principal holding concerned evidentiary uncertainty and the need for a factual basis sufficient to support conviction or acquittal. It should not be mischaracterized as a new, standalone ruling on the substantive legality of sassywood. Nevertheless, the case illustrates the human consequences and evidentiary difficulties that may arise when death and serious accusations become entangled with ordeal practices.

Read together, these decisions show a sustained judicial concern with the illegality, coercion, and danger associated with trial by ordeal. They also remind us that the administration of justice must be based on lawful authority, reliable evidence, and fair procedures—not on fear, superstition, or physical endurance.

4. Constitutional Supremacy, Customary Law, and Human Rights

The 1986 Constitution establishes the supremacy of the Constitution and makes it binding on all persons and authorities. Article 2 provides the foundation for constitutional supremacy. Article 20(a) protects against deprivation of life, liberty, security, property, and other rights except through a hearing and judgment consistent with due process. Article 21(c) protects the rights of persons suspected or accused of crimes, including the right to remain silent. Article 65 vests judicial power in the Supreme Court and subordinate courts and recognizes the application of statutory and customary law in accordance with standards enacted by the Legislature.

These provisions must be read together. Recognition of customary law does not mean that every customary practice is immune from constitutional scrutiny. Customary law operates within the constitutional order. Where a practice conflicts with constitutional guarantees, the Constitution supplies the governing standard.

Human-rights principles reinforce this conclusion. A person accused of wrongdoing is entitled to humane treatment, a fair process, and protection from torture or cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment. A ritual that places a person's life or bodily integrity at risk, compels participation, or treats physical suffering as proof of guilt is incompatible with these principles. No individual should be forced to choose between submitting to a dangerous ordeal and facing community condemnation.

It is also important to distinguish the historical judicial decisions from the current constitutional framework. Jedah and Tenteah were decided before the 1986 Constitution. Their holdings must be understood in their own historical legal context, while the present constitutional analysis rests on the 1986 Constitution and contemporary human-rights standards.

5. Why Sassywood Continues to Concern Liberian Society

The persistence of harmful practices cannot be explained by tradition alone. It may be influenced by distrust of formal institutions, limited access to courts, poverty, geographic barriers, weak public legal education, fear of social exclusion, and the authority exercised by influential community figures. Where people believe that formal justice is distant, expensive, or ineffective, they may turn to informal processes even when those processes carry serious risks.

Accusations of witchcraft or other forms of alleged wrongdoing can also be shaped by fear, illness, unexplained misfortune, family conflict, and community tension. In such circumstances, a vulnerable person may become the target of suspicion. An ordeal can transform an unproven allegation into a public verdict, while denying the accused the safeguards that a fair process requires.

Reports by international human-rights bodies have documented concerns about harmful traditional practices in Liberia, including trial by ordeal. Such reports should be read with attention to their dates and evidentiary limits; they do not establish the present prevalence of every practice. Their importance lies in documenting the risks and the need for reliable monitoring, public education, and effective protection for those threatened by coercion or violence.

6. Rethinking the Liberian Mindset: Tradition Must Evolve

A society's progress depends partly on its willingness to question practices that may once have been accepted without abandoning the values that give the society its identity. Respect for elders, traditional authorities, community solidarity, and cultural heritage can remain important features of Liberian life. But respect should not require silence when a practice threatens another person's life or dignity.

Tradition is not weakened simply because it is examined. Indeed, traditions that can withstand careful reflection are more likely to remain meaningful across generations. The question should not be whether a practice is old, but whether it is just, humane, and compatible with the society's constitutional commitments.

Modernization should therefore not be understood as the rejection of everything customary. It should be understood as the capacity to distinguish between cultural values worth preserving and harmful methods that should be abandoned. Community reconciliation, restorative dialogue, consensual mediation, and restitution may contribute to social peace. Coercive ordeals, forced confessions, physical punishment, and accusations resolved through dangerous rituals should not.

Changing the mindset requires more than legislation. It requires sustained public education in schools, communities, religious institutions, and traditional leadership structures. It requires explaining that a person accused of wrongdoing is still entitled to dignity and lawful treatment. It also requires leaders to demonstrate that community authority is strongest when exercised responsibly and within the law.

7. Preserving Culture Without Preserving Harm

Traditional leaders should be treated as partners in reform, not automatically as opponents of it. Their influence can help communicate legal standards, prevent violence, encourage voluntary settlement, and direct serious allegations to competent authorities. Government institutions, civil society, educators, and community organizations should work with traditional authorities to develop clear boundaries between acceptable customary processes and unlawful practices.

At the same time, the formal justice system must become more accessible and credible. Public education will have limited effect if people who reject an ordeal have nowhere safe and practical to turn. Police, prosecutors, courts, legal aid providers, and local administrators need workable referral mechanisms and the capacity to respond promptly when a person is threatened or harmed.

The regulatory framework also deserves careful review. The United Nations Mission in Liberia and the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights have previously identified tension in the Hinterland Regulations: Article 73 was reported to prohibit sassywood while also referring to other forms of ordeal and historically providing for the licensing of ordeal practitioners. The current text, legal status, and implementation of these provisions should be verified against authoritative records. Any remaining legal ambiguity should be addressed so that the law does not appear to prohibit a dangerous practice while tolerating other forms of coercive or harmful ordeal.

Finally, protection and accountability must be real. Allegations of forced ingestion, assault, confinement, threats, or death connected to an ordeal should be investigated through lawful procedures. Victims and witnesses should be protected from retaliation. No community leader, public official, or private individual should be permitted to use customary authority as a shield against the ordinary operation of criminal law.

8. The Responsibility of a Changing Society

The transformation of Liberian society is not the responsibility of government alone. Families, traditional leaders, religious institutions, teachers, students, lawyers, journalists, and community organizations all influence how people understand justice and cultural identity. Each has a role in promoting a society where disputes can be resolved without violence and where accusations are tested through evidence rather than ritual.

Legal institutions must enforce the law consistently. Educational institutions should teach constitutional values and critical thinking. Traditional leaders should help preserve constructive customs while rejecting coercive practices. The media should report responsibly, avoiding sensationalism and protecting the dignity of affected persons. Citizens should be encouraged to question harmful conduct without treating every criticism of a custom as an attack on Liberian identity.

The broader lesson is that cultural continuity and legal development are not necessarily opposing forces. A society can retain its languages, ceremonies, histories, community structures, and customary forms of reconciliation while refusing to expose people to preventable harm. The measure of a living culture is not its refusal to change, but its capacity to adapt while retaining what is humane and valuable.

9. Conclusion: The Liberia We Choose to Build

Sassywood presents Liberia with a question that reaches beyond one customary practice. It asks whether inherited beliefs should remain unquestioned when they conflict with human dignity, individual liberty, and the rule of law. The Supreme Court's decisions in Jedah and Tenteah provide a clear historical legal foundation against trial by ordeal, while Walker-Nagbe demonstrates the grave factual and human consequences that may accompany such events.

Liberia's future does not require the abandonment of its cultural heritage. It requires the courage to distinguish heritage from harm. We can respect traditional leadership without accepting unlawful coercion. We can preserve community-based reconciliation without allowing dangerous ordeals. We can value our history while insisting that every person—regardless of accusation, status, gender, age, or community—deserves protection under the law.

A modern Liberia must be a society in which justice is not determined by the ability to survive a ritual, but by evidence, fair hearing, lawful authority, and respect for human dignity. That is not a rejection of Liberian identity. It is part of the work of building a more just and humane Liberia.
